## Assignment Framing

Treat this as a CRO case study, not just a visual redesign.

We do not have Wellbeing Nutrition's GA4, Shopify analytics, heatmaps, session recordings, or checkout data. Therefore all recommendations should be framed as heuristic and competitive CRO hypotheses, not proven performance claims.

Do not say "this will increase conversion by X%." Say that the expected impact should be validated through controlled testing.

## Core Thesis

The current PDP already has strong product substance: customer reviews, science, lab reports, traceability, ingredient quality, pack options, and a sticky purchase bar.

The main issue is not lack of content. The issue is purchase architecture.

Final thesis:

> Reduce cognitive load. Move proof closer to purchase. Sell the routine, not just the tub.

The shopper should be able to answer four questions quickly:

- Will this work for my goal?
- Can I trust it?
- How long do I need to take it?
- Which pack should I buy?

## Primary CRO Opportunity

The pack selector is the clearest commercial opportunity.

Current behavior:

- Pack of 1, 2, 3, and 4 are presented mostly as quantity choices.
- All appear to have similar discounting, so there is little reason to commit to more packs.
- The product recommends daily use over a longer routine, but that routine is not clearly connected to pack selection.

Strategic change:

> Change the customer question from "How many tubs?" to "How long do I want to stay consistent?"

Use regimen-based framing:

| Pack | Framing | Duration | Price |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 tub | Starter | about 25 days | Rs. 1,439 |
| 2 tubs | Build the Routine | about 50 days | Rs. 2,878 |
| 3 tubs | Consistency | about 75 days | Rs. 4,318 |
| 4 tubs | 12-Week Routine | about 100 days | Rs. 5,757 |

Serving math:

- 200 g tub / 8 g serving = about 25 servings per tub.
- 4 tubs = about 100 servings, enough to cover a 12-week daily routine.

Do not invent stronger discounts in the prototype. Progressive multi-pack discounts can be recommended as a future test only if margin permits.

## Final CRO Priorities

### 1. Turn Quantity Selection Into Regimen Selection

Make pack cards communicate duration and commitment, not just tub count.

Priority message:

> For best results, the brand recommends daily use for 12 weeks.

The 4-pack can be highlighted as the "12-Week Routine" because it supports the full regimen. Avoid fake "Most Popular" labels unless there is actual sales data.

### 2. Simplify Above-the-Fold Hierarchy

The purchase area should follow a clear order:

1. Product name.
2. Rating/review count.
3. Short benefit-led proposition.
4. Proof chips.
5. Price and daily-cost framing.
6. Pack selector.
7. Routine rationale.
8. Add to Cart / Buy It Now.
9. Delivery and payment reassurance.

Proof chips to surface near the CTA:

- Korean deep-sea sourced.
- Less than 1000 Da peptides.
- Mercury free.
- Heavy-metal tested.
- No added sugar / clean formulation, if supported by the source PDP.

### 3. Connect Science to the Purchase Moment

The PDP has strong science, but much of it is too deep or too academic for the first decision.

Use a simple proof sequence:

- Benefit first.
- Mechanism second.
- Evidence third.

Science cards should lead with shopper-relevant topics:

- Skin hydration.
- Wrinkles and elasticity.
- Skin texture.
- Joint support.

Then provide the study link or citation for users who want depth.

### 4. Move Decision-Relevant Proof Closer to CTA

Bring the proof that reduces immediate hesitation into the purchase module:

- Purity: mercury free and heavy-metal tested.
- Science: ingredients backed by clinical research.
- Routine: one scoop daily.
- Taste: truly unflavoured / no fishy taste, supported by customer review language.
- Convenience: mixes into water, coffee, smoothies, or shakes.

Full science, traceability, lab reports, testimonials, and FAQs can remain lower on the page.

### 5. Make Lower PDP Easier To Scan

Recommended lower-page order:

1. Why this collagen.
2. How to use / daily routine.
3. Science behind the ingredients.
4. Traceability and testing.
5. Customer proof.
6. Lab reports.
7. FAQ.
8. Long-form educational or SEO content.

Every section should answer a buying objection, not merely exist because the brand has content.

## Trust and Claim Consistency Findings

These are good deck insights because they show CRO is also about credibility.

### Absorption Claims

The messy audit notes identified multiple absorption messages across the PDP, such as 1.5x, 4x, and 5x. Even if each has a valid context, the shopper may not understand the differences.

Recommendation:

> Standardize the absorption story and make the comparator/source explicit.

Use one substantiated claim consistently, or use softer language such as "designed for high bioavailability" if the brand does not want to emphasize a multiplier.

### Clinical Proof Language

Prefer precise claim language:

- "Ingredients backed by clinical studies" is clearer than a broad "clinically proven" claim if the evidence is ingredient-led.
- "Manufactured in a US FDA-registered facility" is clearer than wording that could imply product approval.

### Allergy and Source Clarity

If the product is fish-derived, avoid any broad "allergen-free" implication unless legally and scientifically substantiated. If allergy warnings exist elsewhere, keep the language consistent.

## Things We Are Deliberately Not Doing

- Not inventing free gifts, consultations, or diet plans.
- Not claiming guaranteed conversion uplift.
- Not adding unsupported medical claims.
- Not radically rebranding Wellbeing Nutrition.
- Not removing the science content.
- Not copying OZiva or HK Vitals.
- Not making a broad mobile redesign the center of the proposal.
- Not pretending to have internal analytics.
